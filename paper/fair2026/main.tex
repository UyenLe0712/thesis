\begin{abstract}
A GUI agent outputs a coordinate for a machine to click; we train a model for a different
consumer, a person who must be told in one sentence which on-screen element to touch. Does
supervision built automatically from recorded episodes teach a
small vision-language model to write such sentences, and does it help to make the model
\emph{name the target element in a structured slot before writing the sentence}? We
fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B with QLoRA on $64{,}567$ automatically labelled steps and score
$4{,}463$ held-out touch steps by \emph{executability}: the sentence goes to an
independent grounding model that never sees the gold coordinate, and the step is correct
when the returned point falls in the cell of the element the user touched. The first
answer is affirmative and replicated --- $47.6\%$ to $59.4\%$ over two seeds, against a
human-reference level of $75.7\%$ and a contentless-sentence floor of $12.0\%$, the gap
being $22\times$ the seed-to-seed noise and keeping its sign under a second, independently
trained grounder. The second cannot be completed: the descriptor-first branch scores
$57.2\%$ at its single seed, $1.93$ points below the matched control, and the
pre-registered two-seed estimand was abandoned for budget reasons, leaving the comparison
inside the band declared inconclusive in advance. What we can report is a mechanism:
crossing the model's own descriptor against gold labels, the component adds $5.7$ points
where it identifies the element correctly and subtracts $25.2$ where it does not, while the
references still reach $66.3\%$ in that failing cell, so difficulty alone does not account
for the collapse. A slice registered before any treatment score localises a third
of the difference in the $7.3\%$ of steps emitting no descriptor, where the culprit is
fine-tuning itself: the untuned model names the correct action class on $83.4\%$ of them
against $55.1\%$ and $38.8\%$ for the two trained branches. A second stage aimed at that
mechanism was then run --- a preference objective over minimal descriptor pairs, against a
supervised control matched update for update --- and adds $+0.63$ pp $[+0.16, +1.10]$,
again inside the inconclusive band, with $78\%$ of the stage's total gain belonging to the
control rather than to the preference term; a registered variant sourcing its negatives
from the model's own errors died at its feasibility gate, those errors being bipolar rather
than locally confusable. Structured-prefix supervision, on this evidence, does not fail
uniformly; its value is governed by identification accuracy, and it amplifies an error the
fine-tuning stage introduces.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Given a mobile screenshot and a user goal, a GUI agent predicts an action such as
\texttt{click(872,141)}. A different consumer of the same input is a person who needs to
be told what to do: \emph{tap the pencil icon at the top right of the note}. Systems
serving that consumer are appearing in accessibility and user-support
settings~\cite{guideme}, but the generation problem has had little quantitative treatment,
because the output is a sentence: a recorded touch coordinate can be compared with a
predicted one, whereas a sentence cannot be compared with a single reference, many
wordings identifying the same button and no dataset enumerating them.

This paper is a model study and needs an instrument before it can be one. We use
\emph{executability}: the generated sentence goes to a separate grounding
model~\cite{uground} that never sees the gold coordinate, and the step counts as correct
when that model lands in the cell of the element the user touched and the action class
agrees. Section~\ref{sec:metric} gives the calibration that licenses it --- adequacy gate,
measured ceiling, measured floor, sensitivity over five hit rules --- in compressed form;
the instrument is characterised at length elsewhere.

Two model questions are then asked in the order they should be asked.

\emph{Does automatically constructed supervision work at all?} We build training
targets from AndroidControl~\cite{androidcontrol} with no human annotation and no
labels generated by another language model, and fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B~\cite{qwen25vl}
with QLoRA~\cite{lora,qlora}. It does: $47.6 \to 59.4$ points, at two seeds, with the
gap surviving six alternative explanations, one of which discards every sentence that
reproduces its reference verbatim.

\emph{Does it help to name the element before speaking?} The referring-expression
tradition says a description is good when a listener recovers the referent from
it~\cite{mao2016,luo2017,yu2017}, and the residual errors of the fine-tuned model are of
that kind: of its $1{,}824$ failures, $86\%$ carry the correct action and a wording the
grounder cannot resolve. So we registered a branch whose target is a structured descriptor
--- role, name, coordinate, distinguishing cue --- followed by the same scored sentence,
byte for byte, as the control. The registered estimand averages two seeds per branch and
one of the four runs was cancelled for budget reasons, permanently; at the single seed
available the treatment sits $1.93$ points below its control, inside the band declared
inconclusive. The mechanism is nonetheless legible: the component works where the model
identifies the element correctly and costs heavily where it does not, the largest locus of
that cost being a failure fine-tuning introduces before any descriptor exists. A second
registered stage, a minimal-pair preference objective aimed at identification itself, was
then run against a matched supervised control: $+0.63$ pp, inside the inconclusive band
again, $78\%$ of the stage's gain belonging to the control.

Our contributions are: (i) a fully automatic supervision pipeline for GUI instruction
generation, with the resulting fine-tuning gain measured at two seeds and replicated
under a second grounder; (ii) a pre-registered ablation of descriptor-first
supervision, reported against its own registered reading rules including the case
where those rules refuse a conclusion; and (iii) a diagnosis, exploratory and at one
seed, that separates what the structured prefix does from what fine-tuning does, names
the failure a follow-up objective must repair, and reports what happened when we ran
that objective.

\section{Related Work}

\emph{GUI grounding and agents.} AndroidControl~\cite{androidcontrol} provides
$15{,}283$ human-recorded episodes with per-step instructions and gold touch
coordinates; AndroidInTheWild~\cite{aitw} is the source of the $14\%$ tolerance
convention. Grounding models such as SeeClick~\cite{seeclick}, OS-Atlas~\cite{osatlas},
UGround~\cite{uground} and UI-Venus~\cite{uivenus} map a text expression to a screen
point; we use two as instruments, not as baselines. In Aguvis~\cite{aguvis} a model emits
a low-level instruction, but only as an intermediate representation en route to an action,
and its quality is never scored. In AndroidControl the human-written per-step instruction
is an \emph{input} conditioning action prediction; here it is the generation target and
the only scored output. Supplying a structured element list is likewise established ---
the AndroidControl authors fine-tune with an accessibility-derived list as input,
Mind2Web~\cite{mind2web} trains over noisy candidates, Widget
Captioning~\cite{widgetcap} puts the view hierarchy at the input --- so we claim no
novelty for that layer.

\emph{Discriminative description.} Producing a description that identifies one object
among distractors is referring-expression generation~\cite{mao2016}, and later work
places a comprehension model inside the training loop to enforce
discriminativeness~\cite{luo2017,yu2017}. The closest supervised work in the GUI domain is
Widget Captioning~\cite{widgetcap}, which identifies the problem of visually identical
widgets but trains with plain cross-entropy. Our claim is narrow: discriminativeness placed
in the \emph{training target} for GUI instructions, and measured. Outside the GUI domain, targets making a model emit grounding coordinates before
answering help when \emph{trained} rather than prompted~\cite{gcot}, the closest prior to
our branch and the expectation our result should be read against. Inside the GUI domain,
Aguvis reports that removing the inner monologue from its training targets costs $11.4$
points on AndroidControl-Low~\cite{aguvis} --- an intermediate representation consumed by
an action head, not a sentence handed to a person, so it sets an expectation without
settling it.

\emph{Reference-free evaluation.} Verifying generated text against an external
structured source rather than the generator's own beliefs is the principle behind
ALOHa~\cite{aloha}; perturbation checklists with pre-declared thresholds are an
established way of validating a metric~\cite{sai2021}. Two cautions apply: automatic
proxies for human judgement are easily over-trusted~\cite{clark2021}, and grounder
accuracy measured on one ``best'' instruction per element overstates what these models can
do~\cite{groundersunderstand}. The term \emph{executability} is used differently in open
grounded planning~\cite{ogp}, as the share of plans whose every action appears in a given
action library --- a symbolic lookup over text. Ours is behavioural and visual.

\section{Task, Supervision, and Branches}
\label{sec:data}

The task is: given one screenshot $I$ and a user goal $q$, produce a single instruction
$s$ in natural language identifying the element to be touched and the action to
perform, addressed to a human reader.

\emph{Supervision.} No public redistribution of AndroidControl carries the
human-written instruction, the screenshot and the accessibility tree together, so we
join two mirrors on \texttt{(episode\_id, step\_id)} and take accessibility trees for
$99{,}131$ screens from the same release. An off-by-one join would corrupt every downstream
label while leaving the pipeline functional, so it is tested: OCR text at the gold touch
point appears in the reference on $48\%$ of $n=400$ sampled steps against $20\%$ under a
deliberately shifted control. The corpus holds $64{,}567$
training and $6{,}958$ test steps over $12{,}895$ and $1{,}432$ tasks, about $64\%$ of
each being touch steps and forming the scoring population; the split is held out by
task with zero task overlap, verified at full scale. Only $12.6\%$ of accessibility
elements carry any name~\cite{chen2020}, so every screenshot is passed through OCR,
used both as an input feature and as a name source. The descriptor label at each touch
step is built by rule from the smallest accessibility box containing the gold point:
role from the tree, name from the tree when it passes a validity gate and from OCR
inside the box otherwise, the gold point on a $[0,1000]$ grid, and a distinguishing cue.
At full scale $73.6\%$ of labels carry a clear name, $22.0\%$ none, and $7.6\%$ of
target elements share their name with another element on the same screen. The
construction, its two filtering gates and its label-quality audit are the subject of a
companion paper~\cite{companionvcl}; this paper uses the output and reports only what
constrains the model results.

\emph{Branches.} Four datasets are built from identical inputs and differ only in the
generation target: \textbf{S1}, the sentence alone; \textbf{S2}, descriptor then
sentence; \textbf{S2r}, a false descriptor taken from a different screen with
randomised coordinates, token-length matched per example; and \textbf{S2-nopoint}, S2
without the coordinate slot. An S2 target reads
\texttt{<desc>tappable text | Search here | <point>440,91</point> | just above the text
"Coffee"</desc>} followed by the same scored sentence as S1. Nine structural
invariants are checked after every rebuild and all nine hold at full scale; the one
that matters most here is that the scored sentence is byte-identical across branches on
all $64{,}567$ examples, so a difference between branches is a difference in what
precedes the sentence and in nothing else. S2r and S2-nopoint were registered as
attribution controls and, for the budget reasons of Section~\ref{sec:design}, were not
run; we mark them as registered-and-unrun rather than dropping them from the record.

\section{Descriptor-First Supervision}
\label{sec:method}

The intervention is a change of target, not of architecture, input or inference cost.
Both branches read the same prompt --- goal, the reference instructions of the previous
three steps, and the OCR strings --- and both are scored on the sentence alone, with any
descriptor stripped before scoring. S2 differs only in that the target begins with four
slots: \emph{role} (button, text field, list item), \emph{name} (the displayed string),
\emph{coordinate} (the gold touch point rescaled to $[0,1000]$), and a
\emph{distinguishing cue} (the nearest OCR string outside the box with its relative
direction).

Two design choices are not free. The coordinate slot is included because it is a clean
label at zero construction cost, and the evidence available at design time indicated that
intermediate steps carrying an explicit point help where free-form verbal reasoning hurts.
The descriptor is emitted, not consumed: the model must produce its own identification
before it writes, which makes the branch an ablation of \emph{discriminative commitment}
rather than of extra input. That commitment cuts both ways, as Section~\ref{sec:diag}
tests: a model that has just written the right name and point is well placed to write a
sentence about that element, and one that has written the wrong ones has bound itself to
them.

\section{The Instrument}
\label{sec:metric}

\subsection{Definition}
Let $s$ be the generated sentence with any descriptor removed, $I$ the screenshot, $g$
the gold touch coordinate, and $E$ the set of accessibility elements on the screen after
merging elements whose centres lie within $24$\,dp. Let $G$ be a grounding model,
independent of the model under test and without access to $g$, returning $p = G(I,s)$.
The step is correct when all three hold: \emph{(i) action}, the canonicalised action
types of $s$ and of the reference agree, twelve surface verbs mapping to one touch class
and touch, type, scroll, long-press and back remaining distinct; \emph{(ii) polarity},
$s$ and the reference do not name opposite states of the same control, checked against
nine toggle pairs; \emph{(iii) localisation}, $p$ lies within the conventional
tolerance $|p_x - g_x| \le 0.14W$, $|p_y - g_y| \le 0.14H$~\cite{aitw} \emph{and} no
competing element centre is closer to $p$ than $g$ itself. The tolerance region is a
per-axis rectangle, not a disc ($\pm151$ px horizontally against $\pm336$ px vertically
on a $1080\times2400$ screen), and the site of the target cell is the recorded touch
point, not the centre of the box containing it. Condition (iii) refines the tolerance
test and never accepts a step that test rejects, so the two are not alternatives.
Executability is the proportion of touch steps satisfying all three.

The rubric is a recorded human touch coordinate, not a model's opinion, so this is not
LLM-as-judge: $G$ contributes a localisation and geometry against $g$ decides. The same
$G$ scores every branch, which removes any bias acting equally on all of them.

Fig.~\ref{fig:cell} shows a step on which the two rules disagree. The choice is not
cosmetic: against a sentence naming the wrong button the tolerance test still awards
credit in $84.3\%$ of cases, a dynamic range of some $16$ points, where the cell rule
awards $2.8\%$ while holding a ceiling of $99.7\%$ under a $3\%$ injected offset. We
report the tolerance score alongside the headline throughout, never as the headline.

\subsection{Calibration}
Four measurements license the readings in this paper.

\emph{Adequacy.} A pass condition of median localisation error at most $3\%$ of screen
width was fixed before any compute was spent, derived from screen geometry: the
tolerance margin --- half the distance from the target to the nearest other element after
merging --- has median $6.2\%$ of width and 10th percentile $3.6\%$. UGround-V1-2B
attains $0.7\%$ on $300$ test steps. The gate passes, but the error distribution is
bimodal (the grounder either lands near the centre or elsewhere entirely), so a
percentile condition would have been the better choice, and one declared
installation-fault detector fired: on $30.7\%$ of steps the horizontal answer is exactly
screen centre, part data and part the grounder abandoning the axis when it cannot
identify an element. That depresses scores rather than inflating them.

\emph{Ceiling.} Passing every human-written reference through the same pipeline scores
$\mathbf{75.7\%}$ $[74.1, 77.3]$ over all $4{,}463$ touch steps. Every score
below is read against $75.7$, not against $100$. An earlier estimate from a $300$-step
subsample gave $70.0\%$, five points low; that number is withdrawn.

\emph{Floor.} Replacing every sentence with the contentless \emph{tap the button} scores
$12.0\%$ $[9.7, 14.4]$ on a random $800$-step slice whose ceiling is $74.9\%$, so the
usable range is $62.9$ points and the metric is not carried by a visual prior. A
genuine reference sentence written for a \emph{different} step --- correct register,
matched length, wrong content --- scores lower still, $6.1\%$ $[4.5, 7.9]$, on disjoint
intervals: a wrong sentence actively misleads the instrument where an empty one leaves
it to a weak prior. This also disposes of the reading that the metric rewards
annotator style, since style is exactly what that control preserves.

\emph{What the metric weighs.} On the same slice, keeping the locative clause and
replacing the element name with the generic \emph{the item} costs $28.5$ points on the
$193$ steps it touches ($b{=}58$, $c{=}3$, $p<0.001$), against $3.5$ points for deleting
the locative clause and keeping the name on a near-identical population of $198$
($b{=}8$, $c{=}1$, $p{=}0.046$). Naming the element is worth roughly eight times saying
where it is. Meaning-preserving rewrites of the references --- verb substitution,
fronting the locative clause, both at once --- move executability by $+0.35$ pp
($[-0.59, +1.29]$) over the $1{,}139$ steps they alter, with $2.6\%$ of steps changing
verdict, so the instrument is insensitive to phrasing while being sensitive to content.

\emph{Why not a reference-based score.} Scored with a content-word F1 against the single
reference, $52.8\%$ of the sentences the metric accepts from the untuned model fall below
$0.5$, against $13.9\%$ for the fine-tuned one: a reference-based score charges the two
arms unequally for register, a property unrelated to identification. BLEU-4 and ROUGE-L
happen to rank the arms in the same order ($9.7/40.3$ for Base against $38.7/67.3$ for
S1), and we draw nothing from that agreement. What they cannot do is locate the remaining
room: the references score $100$ on ROUGE-L by construction, where executability puts them
at $75.7$.

\subsection{Robustness of the instrument}
Choosing a hit rule is a researcher degree of freedom, so we measured what the choice
buys. Rescoring three arms on $698$ steps under five rules --- Euclidean disc, per-axis
rectangle, the cell rule as implemented, a cell rule seeded at the box centre, and a
nearest-box rule --- gives ceilings of $80.2$, $82.2$, $74.2$, $56.6$ and $82.2$ and a
fine-tuning gain of $+12.6$, $+13.0$, $+11.7$, $+9.5$ and $+13.0$ points. The absolute
level moves by up to $26$ points; the ordering never moves and the gain stays between
$9.5$ and $13.0$. The instrument contributes no variance of its own: on the $2{,}810$
steps where two training runs emitted byte-identical sentences the grounder returned
identical coordinates and identical verdicts, $2{,}810$ of $2{,}810$.

Intervals are nonparametric cluster bootstraps with $10{,}000$ resamples clustered by
application ($G = 1{,}091$ clusters, $G_{\text{eff}} = 454.3$ under Kish's
formula~\cite{kish1965}), the clustering rule fixed in advance because it determines the
available power~\cite{cameron2008}. The measured design effect is $1.10$, giving a
minimum detectable paired effect of $2.2$ points at $80\%$ power. That figure prices
instrument noise alone. A contrast read across two \emph{training runs} also carries the
seed term of Section~\ref{sec:design}, so Section~\ref{sec:mindesc} reads its result
against the corresponding single-seed design figure instead of against this one.

\section{Experimental Design and Pre-Registration}
\label{sec:design}

All branches fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct~\cite{qwen25vl} with
QLoRA~\cite{lora,qlora} under LLaMA-Factory~\cite{llamafactory}: 4-bit NF4, LoRA rank $8$,
$\alpha = 16$, dropout $0.05$, adapters on \texttt{q,k,v,o,gate,up,down}, vision tower and
projector frozen, context $2560$, effective batch $16$, learning rate $1\times10^{-4}$,
cosine schedule, two epochs, on a single A100 ($8{,}072$ steps, $\approx 23$ h). One
configuration file serves every branch, three keys changing in it --- dataset, seed, output
directory --- and each run archives the file it used; the trainable parameter count was
verified arithmetically at $14{,}966{,}784$, confirming the vision tower stayed frozen.
Losses on an L4 and an A100 agree to three digits with identical \texttt{total\_flos}, and
generation at batch size $1$ and $8$ is byte-identical.

Inference is greedy in every arm, one sentence per step, the descriptor stripped by a fixed
regular expression before scoring so no arm is scored on text the others cannot produce;
the untuned arm reads the same prompt template. The prompt supplies the goal, the
human-written instructions of the previous three steps and the OCR strings, byte-identical
across branches and between training and inference. Because the history carries
human-written sentences rather than an action log, scoring is teacher-forced on context:
absolute scores are conditioned on that, branch differences are not.

The design was committed to version control before any training run and fixes the reading
of all four outcomes in advance: a $\Delta$ whose interval excludes zero and exceeds the
seed noise floor is positive; one whose interval touches zero is a trend; an interval
covering zero with $|\Delta|$ below the detectable effect is inconclusive; a negative upper
bound is harm. The registered quantity is
$\Delta = \mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S2}) - \mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S1})$ over \emph{two seeds
per branch}, the operative threshold set at $2.8$ pp --- the bootstrap standard error of
the paired difference, $0.38$ pp, with a seed term of $0.46$ pp doubled because it rests on
one degree of freedom --- and the inconclusive band at $-2.8 \ldots +1.7$ pp. That $2.8$ pp
is the band rule for $\mathrm{S2} - \mathrm{S1}$ and is deliberately conservative; the
$2.11$ pp figure Section~\ref{sec:mindesc} uses to read $\mathrm{MIN} - \mathrm{CE2}$
combines the same two variance components without that doubling. Amendments are
appended with dates, never edited in place; twenty-seven are recorded, twenty-one before
any executability score existed. We flag rather than hide the pattern in the six that came
later: each moved the room upward and the threshold downward, and the first also moved
against us, cutting the control branch's share of the ceiling from $84.4\%$ to $78.4\%$.

One departure is stated here rather than in a footnote. \textbf{The second seed of the
treatment branch was never run}, a budget decision taken on 23 August 2026, and it will not
be. The registered estimand is permanently incomplete, and no later analysis upgrades the
single-seed comparison into the conclusion the two-seed rule would have licensed.

\subsection{Automatic supervision works, and the effect is replicated}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the picture. Fine-tuning on supervision that cost no human
annotation moves the model from $47.6$ to $59.4$ points, closing $42\%$ of the distance
to the human-reference level. Paired over the same steps (McNemar), S1 over Base is
$+11.5$ points at seed 101 ($b = 284$, $c = 798$, $\chi^2 = 243.2$) and $+12.0$ at seed
202, both $p<0.001$, where $b$ counts steps the lower arm resolves and the higher one
does not.

All rates are over the same denominator of $4{,}463$ touch steps. Two runs each emitted
one empty sentence, S1 on one step and S2 on another; both are scored zero rather than
dropped, since a model's refusal to answer is exactly what the metric exists to catch.

That number is flattered by the quarter of steps on which S1 reproduces the reference's
content words: restricted to the $3{,}367$ steps where the model writes its own
sentence, the arms read $44.7$, $53.1$ and $75.0$ and the fraction closed is $28\%$,
with the gap still $+8.4$ points ($b=264$, $c=547$, $p<0.001$). Five further readings of
the per-step record leave the ordering intact: scoring localisation alone leaves $+11.3$
points, and the untuned model in fact names
the correct action class \emph{more} often ($96.5\%$ against $94.4\%$); the gain is
$+15.0$ points on a task's opening step against $+11.0$ afterwards, on overlapping
intervals; screen difficulty by element count gives $+12.6$, $+9.5$, $+12.4$;
per-cluster, S1 wins $395$ clusters, ties $565$ and loses $131$, reproduced at the second
seed; and there is no home-field advantage, S1 scoring $59.1\%$ on applications seen in
training, $59.0\%$ on unseen ones and $59.2\%$ where none can be attributed.

\emph{Retraining noise.} A gap between systems is only meaningful against the movement
caused by rebuilding one. Retraining S1 under a second seed shifts the score by $+0.5$
points, a paired interval of $[-0.2, +1.3]$ containing zero, McNemar $p = 0.19$. The
sharper contrast is churn: the seeds disagree on $6.4\%$ of steps where the intervention
moves $24.2\%$. Step-level agreement across seeds is $93.6\%$ ($\kappa = 0.867$), but
$63\%$ of those pairs are byte-identical sentences a deterministic instrument must agree
on; on the $1{,}652$ steps where the runs wrote something different it is $82.6\%$
($\kappa = 0.650$), the figure we quote.

\subsection{The descriptor branch}
The registered comparison is $\mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S2}) - \mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S1})$
over two seeds each. With the treatment at one seed, what can be stated is this:
\emph{with Qwen2.5-VL-3B under QLoRA and the present automatic labelling pipeline, the
S2/101 checkpoint scores $1.93$ points below S1/101 ($b = 254$, $c = 340$, interval
$[-3.06, -0.75]$) and $2.44$ points below S1/202 over the full $4{,}463$ steps, the sign
holds under UI-Venus, and the registered estimand remains incomplete.} Against the declared
bands, $-2.19$ pp relative to the two-seed control mean falls inside $-2.8 \ldots +1.7$:
\emph{inconclusive}, not a negative result, and the missing run means it stays there. The
seed noise on the control pair, $\pm1.3$ pp at the interval edge, is most of the reason: a
single run can move by half the observed gap.

What the missing run could have changed is fixed by the registered thresholds. For the
two-seed mean to reach the weakly-positive band --- an edge at $\Delta = +1.7$ pp --- the
cancelled run would have had to score $65.0\%$, $5.4$ points above the best checkpoint we
have measured and above the second grounder's ceiling on its own slice; for the negative
band it would have had to score at or below $56.0\%$, well inside the range a seed change
produces. The remaining outcome space was \emph{inconclusive or negative} and the
budget decision closed it at inconclusive; we report this rather than let a reader infer a
suppressed positive.

Two things do not depend on the missing seed. The branch is far above the untuned model,
$+9.6$ points $[+8.2, +11.1]$, so the descriptor target does not break fine-tuning; and it
emits its coordinate slot well, $71.0\%$ of self-generated points landing inside the $14\%$
window with a median horizontal error of $0.81\%$ of screen width. Whatever the branch
costs, it does not cost the ability to point.

\subsection{A preference objective at the descriptor layer}
\label{sec:mindesc}
Section~\ref{sec:diag} points at identification accuracy rather than at the prefix, so we
ran the objective registered for it: from the S2 checkpoint, $800$ further updates on
minimal pairs whose accepted side is the gold descriptor followed by the sentence and
whose rejected side is a wrong descriptor followed by the \emph{same sentence, verbatim},
so the preference term can be reduced only by choosing the right element. Its attribution
control, CE2, is plain supervised training on the accepted side alone: same checkpoint,
same $800$ updates, same schedule, no preference term.

Both arms are in Table~\ref{tab:main}. The primary registered quantity is the paired
difference between them: $+0.63$ pp, $[+0.16, +1.10]$, $b = 70$, $c = 42$, $\chi^2 = 6.5$,
$p = 0.011$. That interval carries instrument noise only (SE $0.38$ pp),
whereas the minimum detectable effect for a single-seed design, computed in advance from
that same error together with the $0.46$ pp seed term of Section~\ref{sec:design}, is
$2.11$ pp, and $+0.63$ is $1.4\times$ that seed term. By the reading rule fixed
before any score existed this is the \emph{inconclusive} band: separable from measurement
noise, not from training noise.

The decomposition is the part worth carrying forward. The preference arm is $+2.87$ pp above
S2 $[+2.03, +3.76]$, but $+2.24$ $[+1.41, +3.11]$ of that is bought by the control --- $800$
further supervised updates, no preference term anywhere --- and only $+0.63$ sits on top: $78\%$ of the movement belongs to the control arm, and reporting
$+2.87$ as the effect of the objective would overstate it fourfold. The same split appears
one layer down, at the descriptor, where the control lifts joint name-and-point accuracy by
$+5.90$ pp and the preference term by $+0.80$. Nor does the stage overtake plain sentence
supervision: MIN $-$ S1 is $+0.94$ $[-0.09, +2.05]$, $p = 0.11$, CE2 $-$ S1 is $+0.31$,
$p = 0.61$. The stage repairs the damage the descriptor target did to itself, then rises a
little above it.

The pre-registration predicted $+0.35$ pp for this contrast, from a
descriptor-to-executability conversion factor of $0.43$ measured on our own data and
written down before the run; the measurement returned $+0.63$.

\emph{A registered variant the data refused.} The rejected side needs a source, and two
were registered: the heuristic one used here, the nearest same-role element $80$--$350$ px
away, and an on-policy one taking the element the checkpoint itself names when it names the
wrong one. The second was stopped by its feasibility gate before any training, $14{,}000$
training screens giving $459$ usable pairs, $3.3\%$ against a threshold of $25\%$, and the
reason is measurable: over the $3{,}246$ steps carrying a wrong name the distance from the
element named to the target has quartiles of $70$, $351$ and $748$ px, so the window
rejects $76.5\%$. These errors are bipolar --- the right element under another name, or a
different region of the screen --- not the local confusion an on-policy negative presumes,
and the heuristic source does not sample them so much as manufacture a neighbour-confusion
regime the data barely holds. We report all three sources because the heuristic one is
what was left standing after the measurement, not a design preference.

\section{Where the Descriptor Helps and Where It Breaks}
\label{sec:diag}

This section is exploratory and rests on one treatment seed. It is reported because the
mechanism it exposes is what a follow-up objective must act on, and because two of its
components were registered before any treatment score existed.

\subsection{The component works when identification succeeds}

against the gold labels, independently of the sentence. Crossing name correctness against
coordinate correctness on the $3{,}240$ steps carrying a gold name gives

The branch wins or ties in three cells of four. All the damage sits in one: with both the
wrong name and a coordinate outside tolerance --- $738$ steps, $16.5\%$ of the population
--- executability falls to $5.6\%$. Its cost is commitment: having named the wrong element,
the branch writes a sentence faithful to it, while the control, committed to nothing, still
retrieves the right one three times in ten.

The objection is that this stratification conditions on a variable the treatment itself
produces, so the failing cell may simply be the hard steps. The two rightmost columns test
that and are not conditioned on the treatment: if the cell were merely hard the references
would fall with it, yet they reach $66.3\%$ there, $9$ points below their own average, where
the treatment reaches $5.6$ and the untuned model $24.4$. The cell is somewhat harder than
average and that does not account for the collapse; the comparison is not clean --- a
correct test needs a difficulty measure fixed before the treatment ran --- but the confound
is bounded rather than merely acknowledged. One cell does behave as the objection predicts:
\emph{name correct, point outside}, ceiling $31.0\%$, is a region the instrument largely
cannot see, and the near-zero $\Delta$ there is absence of signal, not of effect.

The decomposition of the total is arithmetic and carries no such caveat: $-1.28$ pp from
the $92.7\%$ of steps carrying a descriptor, $-0.63$ pp from the $7.3\%$ that do not.

definitional. What is not definitional is that the control also scores only $19.7\%$.

\subsection{Four alternative explanations tested and set aside}
\emph{Length.} Negative on long sentences ($-1.85$) and short ones ($-2.00$), the two
branches' length distributions coinciding (median $31$ characters each).
\emph{Application slice.} Negative on seen ($-2.25$), unseen ($-6.41$) and unattributable
($-1.59$) steps alike. \emph{Canonicalisation.} The \emph{go back} $\to$ touch collapse in
condition (i) hides exactly this failure mode --- $64$ treatment sentences containing
\emph{go back} pass the action gate where only $21$ are executable --- so we re-ran with
the strict variant, registered on 16 August before any treatment score: the difference
moves from $-1.93$ to $-1.86$, all four arms dropping $0.20$--$0.31$ points with the
ordering unchanged. \emph{Label noise.} On the $26$ steps whose descriptor label is OCR
garbage the difference is $+7.69$ ($p = 0.63$), the opposite direction.

\subsection{Why this does not simply contradict the grounding-first prior}
Results reporting gains for targets that ground before answering are not measured on this
task, and three differences matter. Their supervision is a question--answer pair whose
grounded object is what the question is \emph{about}, so a grounding error shows in the
answer; ours admits a fluent, wrong sentence after a wrong descriptor. Their grounding
target is verified against annotation at training time, ours at inference against the
model's own identification, where Table~\ref{tab:cross} finds the asymmetry. And their
gains are measured on answer accuracy, whose floor is near chance, while executability has
a measured floor of $12.0$ and ceiling of $75.7$, leaving $62.9$ points of range in which
$2$ points is small. Ours is a boundary condition on that prior, not a contradiction: the
prefix pays where the model can identify the referent, and identification with $22.0\%$ of
target elements carrying no name is harder than for a photographed object.

\subsection{What this says about the intervention}
Read together, the three tables say more than ``the ablation did not pay''. The supervision
is not uniformly inert: it adds $5.7$ points where the model identifies the element
correctly and subtracts $25$ where identification has failed, so its net effect is governed
by identification accuracy, not by the prefix. The lever is therefore not ``remove a slot''
--- the coordinate slot carries the weight, $+1.1$ points on its own where the name is wrong
against $+0.5$ for the name alone --- but ``make identification reliable''. Lifting the
failing cell to the control's own level on those $738$ steps would be worth $4.2$ points,
the size of effect the study seeks. Section~\ref{sec:mindesc} reports the objective
registered for exactly that: $+0.63$ pp over its supervised control, inside the
inconclusive band, $78\%$ of the stage's movement belonging to that control. Identification
accuracy is both the lever and, so far, what has not moved far enough. Choosing this
objective \emph{after} seeing the S2 result is a post-hoc decision and is recorded as one.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\emph{The registered estimand is incomplete, permanently.} The treatment branch has one
seed, and so do both stage-2 arms; everything said about them is exploratory. The sentences
the pre-registration forbids --- that the hypothesis was refuted, that the treatment is
harmful by the registered quantity, that a McNemar $p$ settles a question in which seed
variance is a component --- are not said here.

\emph{Ceiling and blind region.} The human-reference level is $75.7\%$, so the room for any
intervention is narrower than a score read against $100$ suggests. The references fail on
$1{,}083$ steps, $71\%$ of those because the grounder misses by more than the tolerance on a
human sentence, and $935$ steps defeat all three arms. The metric also cannot see
displacements below the $63$ px merge radius: it detects naming the wrong element, not
pointing slightly off inside the right one.

\emph{Grounder dependence.} Two grounders agree on the ordering, but both are built on
Qwen-family backbones, so a same-family effect is not excluded, and no human evaluation
anchors either. Our rewrites keep the element name verbatim, so we probed phrasing, not the
referring expression that~\cite{groundersunderstand} perturbs; their $84\%$ is the yield of
an adversarial search over Windows desktops, not a paraphrase failure rate, and the mobile
case they leave open is ours.

\emph{Split composition.} The test split is held out by task, not by application: $95.6\%$
of attributable applications also occur in training, and $55\%$ of test steps cannot be
attributed at all --- \emph{unknown}, never read as \emph{unseen}. No comparison against an external
model is reported standalone, since it would carry a home-field advantage. Nor is task
disjointness phrase disjointness: $17.3\%$ of test references appear verbatim in a
$2.6\%$ slice of the training data.

\emph{Scope of the construct.} Every claim is at the level of a system, never of a single
sentence, and the one setting where this family of measure has been validated against
people recommends a reference-based score for ranking systems even while its reference-free
model correlates best per instruction~\cite{zhao2021}. The metric asks whether a grounding
model reaches the right element, not whether a person would, and a system could climb it by
writing for the grounder --- bounded but not excluded: a sentence naming a screen region
scores $89.5\%$ when the region is right and $3.3\%$ when wrong, so the reward is for being
right about position, not for mentioning it. \emph{Scope:} single screen, English interfaces, one dataset, one 3B
base model, one training configuration.

\section{Conclusion}

We built supervision for GUI instruction generation without human annotation and measured
what it buys: $47.6 \to 59.4$ points of executability at two seeds, against a
human-reference level of $75.7$ and a contentless floor of $12.0$, the effect $22\times$
the seed-to-seed noise and its sign preserved under a second grounder trained on different
data. Whether naming the element before writing the sentence adds anything is a question
the pre-registration answers over two seeds per branch, and one run was cancelled; at the
single seed available the treatment sits $1.93$ points below its control, inside the band
declared inconclusive in advance. The diagnosis is what we carry forward: the prefix is
worth $+5.7$ points where the model identifies the element correctly and $-25$ where it
does not, the largest locus of damage being an action-prior failure fine-tuning introduces
before any descriptor exists. The objective this diagnosis specifies has since been run, a
minimal-pair preference stage against a supervised control matched update for update, and
adds $+0.63$ pp $[+0.16, +1.10]$ --- inside the inconclusive band again, with $78\%$ of the
stage's gain belonging to the control; a variant sourcing negatives from the model's own
errors died at its feasibility gate, those errors being bipolar rather than local. The
mechanism is not broken --- it pays where identification succeeds --- but it is bounded by
identification accuracy, which is where the next experiment must act.
