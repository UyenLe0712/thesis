\begin{abstract}
Given a mobile screenshot and a user goal, we train Qwen2.5-VL-3B to generate one English
instruction for a human reader, with no new annotation. The intervention is a change of
training target: a structured descriptor (role, name, coordinate, distinguishing cue)
precedes the same scored sentence, and a second stage applies ORPO to descriptor-controlled
pairs whose two sides share that sentence verbatim. Sentence-only fine-tuning, the control
for the target change, reaches $59.4\%$ executability averaged over two training runs, up from
$47.6\%$ for the untuned model, against a human-reference score of $75.7\%$ on $4{,}463$ touch
steps. MIN-DESC reaches $60.0\%$ against an accepted-only supervised continuation at $59.4\%$
given the same checkpoint and $800$ updates, both continuing from the descriptor-first stage at
$57.2\%$. A sentence is scored as executable when a separate grounding model, which never sees
the gold coordinate, predicts a point that is within tolerance of the touched element and closer
to it than to any competitor, and the action class matches. Contentless text scores $12.0\%$
and a fluent sentence written for a different screen scores $6.1\%$, both on an $800$-step
slice. Identification accuracy and further runs of the descriptor stages remain the open
experimental variables.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Given a mobile screenshot and a user goal, a GUI agent predicts an action such as
\texttt{click(872,141)}. A person needs a different output, namely the English sentence that
tells them what to touch, for example \emph{tap the pencil icon at the top right of the note}.
Systems that serve such a reader are appearing in accessibility and user-support
settings~\cite{guideme}, yet the generation problem has had little quantitative treatment. A
predicted coordinate can be checked against a recorded one. A sentence cannot, because many
wordings identify the same button and no dataset enumerates them.

We hold the backbone, the prompt and the decoding rule fixed, and change what the model is
trained to emit. The control for that change is sentence-only supervision, with
Qwen2.5-VL-3B~\cite{qwen25vl} fine-tuned by QLoRA~\cite{lora,qlora} on
AndroidControl~\cite{androidcontrol} to predict the per-step instruction the corpus already
records. Descriptor-first supervision places a rule-built role, name, touch point and
distinguishing cue ahead of that sentence, which is held byte-identical to the sentence-only
target. MIN-DESC then continues from the descriptor-first checkpoint. It applies
ORPO~\cite{orpo} to pairs of descriptor-bearing responses whose sentence suffix is shared
verbatim, and its comparison is an accepted-only supervised continuation from the same
checkpoint. Both targets follow the referring-expression criterion that a description succeeds
when a listener recovers the referent~\cite{mao2016,luo2017,yu2017}. The labels are built by
rule from the recorded episodes (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Sentences are scored by executability
(Section~\ref{sec:metric}). Sentence-only supervision scores $59.4\%$ executability and
descriptor-first $57.2\%$; of the two stage-2 procedures, MIN-DESC scores $60.0\%$ against
$59.4\%$ for its accepted-only supervised continuation. Section~\ref{sec:results} reads each
contrast against what one training run per branch can resolve.

Our primary contribution is a controlled target-space training design for GUI instruction
generation. It comprises (i) descriptor-first supervision, in which the model emits a
rule-built role, name, point and distinguishing cue before a scored sentence held
byte-identical to the sentence-only target; and (ii) MIN-DESC, descriptor-controlled ORPO
pairs with a shared sentence suffix, evaluated against an accepted-only continuation from
the same checkpoint for the same $800$ updates. As a secondary contribution, (iii) we use a
calibrated executability instrument to compare these systems. The sentence-only control
rises from $47.6\%$ to $59.4\%$ averaged over two training runs; human references score
$75.7\%$ ($n=4{,}463$), and contentless and wrong-screen controls score $12.0\%$ and $6.1\%$
($n=800$).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\emph{Targets for GUI instruction generation.} AndroidControl~\cite{androidcontrol} records
$15{,}283$ human episodes with a per-step instruction and a gold touch coordinate. Its authors
fine-tune with that instruction and an accessibility-derived element list as \emph{input} to
action prediction, while here the instruction is the generation target and the only scored
output. Supplying a structured element list is not our claim. Mind2Web~\cite{mind2web} ranks
noisy candidates before generation, and Widget Captioning~\cite{widgetcap} conditions on the
view hierarchy. Grounding models map an expression to a screen point~\cite{seeclick,osatlas};
we use UGround~\cite{uground}, trained on this corpus, and UI-Venus~\cite{uivenus}, not trained
on it, as instruments and not as baselines.

\emph{Discriminative description.} Writing a description that picks one object out of its
distractors is referring-expression generation, discriminative by construction since maximum
mutual information~\cite{mao2016} and listener-in-the-loop rewards~\cite{luo2017,yu2017}. The
nearest supervised precedent inside the GUI domain is Widget Captioning~\cite{widgetcap}, which
names visually identical widgets as the hard case yet trains under plain cross-entropy. Our
interventions sit in the training target instead of the decoder or the reward. Outside GUIs,
emitting grounding coordinates ahead of the answer helps when that ordering is trained and not
merely prompted~\cite{gcot}. Stripping the inner monologue from the Aguvis targets costs $11.4$
pp on AndroidControl-Low, our subtraction from their ablation table~\cite{aguvis}; that
intermediate string feeds an action head and is never read by a person.

\emph{Anchoring a score outside the generator.} ALOHa~\cite{aloha} verifies generated text
against a detector rather than the generator's own beliefs, and perturbation checklists are an
established way of validating such a score~\cite{sai2021}. For navigation instructions a
reference-free model correlates best per sentence, while its authors still recommend a
reference-based score when ranking systems~\cite{zhao2021}. Our instrument is neither, so
their validation does not transfer to it, and we restrict every claim to the system level. Untrained panels separate machine text from human text at chance~\cite{clark2021}. The
word \emph{executability} carries a different sense in open grounded planning~\cite{ogp}, in which
it counts plans whose every action appears in a given action library. That is a symbolic lookup
over text, while ours is behavioral and visual.

\section{Task and Data Pipeline}
\label{sec:data}

From a screenshot $I$ and a goal $q$ the model produces one instruction $s$ for a human reader,
naming the element to touch and the action to perform.

\emph{Sources.} No public redistribution of AndroidControl carries instruction, screenshot and
accessibility tree together. We join two mirrors on \texttt{(episode\_id, step\_id)} and take
trees for $99{,}131$ screens from the same release. An off-by-one join would corrupt every
label while leaving the pipeline functional. We test alignment directly: OCR text at the gold
touch point appears in the reference instruction on $48\%$ of $n=400$ sampled steps, against
$20\%$ under a shifted control. The corpus holds $64{,}567$ training and $6{,}958$ test steps
over $12{,}895$ and $1{,}432$ tasks. Its $4{,}463$ test touch steps form the scoring
population. The split is held out by task, with zero task overlap at full scale; overlap between
individual phrases is reported in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\emph{Descriptor labels.} On $120$ screens sampled at random from the $99{,}131$, $12.6\%$ of
elements carry any name, a shortfall reported elsewhere~\cite{chen2020}. Every screenshot
therefore passes through OCR, used as an input feature and a name source. A label is built by
rule from the smallest accessibility box containing the gold point. Role comes from the tree;
the name from the tree when it passes a validity check, from OCR inside the box otherwise.
Coordinates go on a $[0,1000]$ grid; the cue is the nearest OCR string outside the box, with
its direction. Of the $41{,}099$ training labels, $73.6\%$ carry a clear name, $4.4\%$ only a
symbol and $22.0\%$ none. A further $7.6\%$ of target elements share their name with another
element on the same screen. The pipeline adds no new annotation. Two filters run before a label is kept, one rejecting
accessibility strings that carry no readable name and one rejecting boxes covering more than a
quarter of the screen; a manual audit of $1{,}074$ labels checks what survives them.

\emph{Branches.} Four training sets share the prompt and differ in target: \textbf{S1}, the
sentence alone; \textbf{S2}, descriptor then sentence; \textbf{S2r}, a false descriptor from a
different screen with randomized coordinates, token-length matched; and \textbf{S2-nopoint}, S2
without the coordinate slot. Nine structural invariants are rechecked after every rebuild; all
nine hold at full scale. The invariant used later is a byte-identical scored sentence across
branches on all $64{,}567$ examples. S2r and S2-nopoint were registered as attribution controls
and not run (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Targets and a Stage-2 Objective}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Invariants across branches}

The intervention changes the training target, not the model architecture or input. S1 and S2
use the same Qwen2.5-VL-3B backbone, prompt, previous-step history, OCR input and decoding
rule. Evaluation strips any descriptor with a fixed parser and scores only the instruction.
Fig.~\ref{fig:targets} shows the target strings: S1 predicts the sentence; S2 predicts a
descriptor followed by that same sentence; MIN-DESC compares two descriptor-bearing
responses that share the sentence suffix verbatim.

\subsection{Descriptor-first target}

Let $x$ be the screenshot, goal, history and OCR prompt, and let $s$ be the instruction. S1
uses target $y=s$. S2 uses $y=d^{+}\mathbin{\|}s$, where $d^{+}$ records the target element's
role, name, normalized touch point and distinguishing cue. The labels are constructed by
rule from the accessibility tree, OCR and recorded touch point (Section~\ref{sec:data}), with
no new annotation. The sentence $s$ is byte-identical across S1 and S2 on all $64{,}567$
training examples. Thus S2 makes element information an explicit pre-sentence output and
leaves it in the autoregressive context of the sentence. S1 and S2 still differ in prefix
presence, content and length; separating those factors requires the registered S2r and
S2-nopoint controls that were not run.

\subsection{MIN-DESC objective}

MIN-DESC continues from the S2 checkpoint on descriptor-controlled preference pairs. The
accepted response is $y^{+}=d^{+}\mathbin{\|}s$; the rejected response is
$y^{-}=d^{-}\mathbin{\|}s$, where $d^{-}$ describes the nearest different same-role element
$80$--$350$ px away. The role slot and sentence suffix $s$ are shared; name, point and cue may
differ. Holding $s$ verbatim removes sentence-content variation between the two strings, but
does not make its autoregressive likelihood cancel because the sentence remains conditioned
on the preceding descriptor and the descriptors may differ in token length.

We optimize ORPO~\cite{orpo}. With the length-normalized log likelihood
$\ell_\theta(y|x)=|y|^{-1}\sum_t\log p_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})$ and $g(z)=z-\log(1-e^{z})$, the
implemented loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MIN}}=-\ell_\theta(y^{+}|x)
-\beta\log\sigma\!\left[g(\ell^{+})-g(\ell^{-})\right],
\]
with $\beta=0.1$. Prompt tokens are masked, so both log likelihoods run over descriptor and
sentence together; the sentence is inside the preference score. Under the model's tokenizer
the accepted side has median length $44$ tokens against $45$ for the rejected side, with
5th--95th percentiles of $37$--$58$ on both. The median signed difference is zero, and
$62.8\%$ of pairs fall within two tokens. The shared suffix tokenizes identically on both
sides in all $22{,}854$ pairs. No pair is truncated, the longest full sequence being $2{,}002$
tokens against the $2{,}560$ cutoff with image tokens included. A descriptor-length difference
survives, and the pair is not an isolated test of identification.

\subsection{Accepted-only stage-2 control}

CE2 continues supervised training from the same S2 checkpoint on the same $22{,}854$ accepted
examples, with the same seed, effective batch, learning-rate schedule and $800$ optimizer
updates, but without rejected responses or a preference term. CE2 is therefore
update-matched and accepted-data-matched to MIN, not compute-matched: MIN additionally
processes one rejected response per example. MIN$-$CE2 compares the two implemented stage-2
training procedures; it does not isolate a causal effect of the preference term. At the
pinned commit~\cite{llamafactory} the pairwise trainer zeroes every dropout module at
construction, including the LoRA dropout of $0.05$ that the supervised trainer keeps active.
That difference is not controlled.

\section{The Instrument}
\label{sec:metric}

\subsection{Definition}
Let $s$ be the generated sentence with any descriptor stripped, $g$ the recorded touch coordinate
on screenshot $I$, and $G$ a separate grounding model~\cite{uground} that never sees $g$ and
returns $p = G(I,s)$. A step is executable when three conditions hold. The canonicalized action class of $s$ agrees
with the reference, and $s$ names no opposite state of the same control (\emph{turn off} when
the reference turns it on). The predicted point lies inside the conventional per-axis $14\%$
tolerance~\cite{aitw}, that is $|p_x - g_x| \le 0.14W$ and $|p_y - g_y| \le 0.14H$. No other
element center is closer to $p$ than $g$ is. The cell is seeded at the recorded touch
coordinate, not the center of its bounding box, and the last condition refines the tolerance
test rather than replacing it, never admitting a step that test rejects.

The rubric is a recorded human touch coordinate, so this is not LLM-as-judge: $G$ supplies only a location, the comparison against $g$ supplies the verdict, and the same $G$ scores every branch.

We measured what the nearest-center clause buys before adopting it. Table~\ref{tab:inject}
perturbs the grounder's answer on $250$ touch steps along four random angles, which gives
$1{,}000$ draws per rule. When the perturbation lands on the center of the nearest competing
element, the tolerance test still awards credit on $843$ of those draws. The cell rule
awards $2.8\%$ there and holds $99.7\%$ at a $3\%$ offset, a range of $97$ pp against $16$.
The clause is a measured choice and not a stylistic one. We report the tolerance score beside the
headline throughout, never as the headline.

One recorded step shows the two rules disagreeing. The user touched the \emph{Birthday} row,
and the fine-tuned model's sentence sends the grounder into that row's cell. The untuned model
wrote ``Click on the `Gender' section\ldots'', and the grounder followed it to the row above,
$128$ px away. The displacement sits inside the $\pm336$ px vertical tolerance, which the
conventional rule scores as correct and the cell rule does not.

\subsection{Calibration}
A gate fixed before any compute required median localization error below $3\%$ of
screen width. The tolerance margin, half the distance from the element to be touched to the
nearest other element after merging, has median $6.2\%$ of width and 10th percentile $3.6\%$.
UGround-V1-2B attains $0.73\%$ on the $300$ reference-branch steps it was declared
on. Over the $4{,}462$-step intersection the median is $0.67\%$ for the human references, $2.00\%$
for S1/101 and $7.82\%$ for Base, which fails the gate.

Base fails it for a reason internal to its sentences. A sentence that names no element leaves $G$
with nothing to localize, and the degenerate controls below show that same coupling between
sentence content and localization error. We read the Base row as a lower anchor on one instrument,
not as a certified level; the gate certifies the human-reference branch, on which we declared it.

Two properties of the error distribution qualify every absolute number. The distribution is
bimodal, with the grounder landing either close to the element center or nowhere near it, and a
percentile condition would have been the better declared form. On $30.7\%$ of steps the
horizontal answer is exactly screen center, partly a property of the data and partly $G$
abandoning that axis. Both effects lower every branch, the human references included.

Human references through the same pipeline score $75.7\%$ $[74.1, 77.3]$
over all $4{,}463$ touch steps; an earlier $300$-step estimate of $70.0\%$ is withdrawn.

The contentless \emph{tap the button}, substituted for every sentence,
scores $12.0\%$ $[9.7, 14.4]$ on a random $800$-step slice whose human-reference score is
$74.9\%$, leaving a usable range of $62.9$ pp. A reference sentence written for a
different step, correct in register and wrong in content, scores $6.1\%$ $[4.5, 7.9]$ on the same
$800$ steps. The two intervals do not overlap. A wrong sentence sends the grounder somewhere
definite and wrong, and an empty one leaves it to a weak prior.

Replacing the element name with \emph{the item} while keeping the
locative clause costs $28.5$ pp on the $193$ steps it touches ($b{=}3$, $c{=}58$, $p<0.001$).
Deleting that clause and keeping the name costs $3.5$ pp on a near-identical $198$ ($b{=}1$,
$c{=}8$, $p{=}0.046$). Verb substitution and fronting of the locative clause move executability by
$+0.35$ pp $[-0.59, +1.29]$ over the $1{,}139$ steps they alter. The grounder is robust to verb
substitution and to reordering of the clauses, and sensitive to which element is named.

Each probe acts on a subset the instrument finds easier than average, with human-reference
scores running from $76.8\%$ to $90.1\%$ against $74.9\%$ over the whole slice. The robustness
reading holds for verb substitution and clause reordering at those difficulties. We did not
test heavier rewrites of how the element itself is described.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:design}

All branches fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct~\cite{qwen25vl} with QLoRA~\cite{lora,qlora} under
LlamaFactory~\cite{llamafactory}: 4-bit NF4, LoRA rank $8$, $\alpha = 16$, dropout $0.05$,
adapters on \texttt{q,k,v,o,gate,up,down}, vision tower and projector frozen, context $2560$,
effective batch $16$, learning rate $1\times10^{-4}$, cosine schedule, two epochs, $8{,}072$
optimizer steps. The trainable parameter count is $14{,}966{,}784$, confirming the vision tower
stayed frozen. Two accelerators of different types give losses that agree to three digits with
identical \texttt{total\_flos}.

Inference is greedy, with the descriptor removed by a fixed parser. The prompt template is
byte-identical across branches and between training and inference, the untuned model included. The
history slot carries human-written sentences, so scoring is teacher-forced on context; this
conditions absolute scores, and every branch is read under the same history.

We committed the design to version control on 5 August 2026, before any training run. Twenty-seven
dated amendments are recorded, twenty-one before any executability score existed. The registered
quantity averages two runs per branch, and three of the four runs it requires were
completed.

\subsection{Sentence-only supervision and its replication}
Supervision assembled without new annotation moves the model from $47.6\%$ to $59.4\%$,
averaged over two training runs (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Paired over the
same steps (McNemar), S1 over Base is $+11.5$ pp at seed 101 ($b = 284$, $c = 798$,
$\chi^2 = 243.2$) and $+12.0$ pp at seed 202, both $p<0.001$. Here $b$ counts steps the
lower-scoring branch resolves and the other does not. All rates use the same $4{,}463$-step
denominator, and the three empty sentences across branches score zero.

That level is inflated by the quarter of steps on which S1 reproduces the reference's content
words. On the $3{,}367$ steps the model answers in its own words, Base, S1 and the human
references read $44.7\%$, $53.1\%$ and $75.0\%$. The S1$-$Base contrast there is $+8.4$ pp
($b = 264$, $c = 547$, $p<0.001$). Application overlap is not visible in the scores. S1
scores $59.1\%$ on applications seen in training ($n{=}1{,}737$), $59.0\%$ on unseen ones
($n{=}78$) and $59.1\%$ when no application can be attributed ($n{=}2{,}648$). That last
group is unmapped, not unseen, and the unseen cell is too small to resolve a difference of
the size at issue.

\emph{Post-hoc readings.} Three further readings, none of them registered, leave the ordering
in place. Dropping the action-class condition and scoring localization alone leaves S1 over
Base at $+11.3$ pp. The untuned model names the correct action class more often than the
fine-tuned one, $96.5\%$ against $94.4\%$. The contrast therefore does not rest on S1 learning
to guess the action class. Across the $1{,}091$ clusters S1 is higher on $395$, level on $565$
and lower on $131$, with the same ordering at seed $202$.

\emph{Retraining noise.} A contrast between systems must be read against the movement a rebuild of
one system produces on its own. A second training run of S1 shifts the score by $+0.52$ pp, $[-0.2, +1.3]$,
McNemar $p = 0.19$. The runs disagree on $6.4\%$ of steps, against the $24.2\%$ that
fine-tuning moves in either direction. Step-level agreement across runs is $93.6\%$
($\kappa = 0.867$), and $63\%$ of pairs are byte-identical sentences a deterministic
instrument must agree on. On the $1{,}652$ steps on which the runs wrote something different it
is $82.6\%$ ($\kappa = 0.650$), the conditional figure we quote.

\subsection{The descriptor branch}
\label{sec:s2}
S2/101 scores $57.2\%$, $1.93$ pp below S1/101 ($b=254$, $c=340$, $[-3.06,-0.75]$) and
$2.18$ pp below the two-run S1 mean; the sign holds under UI-Venus. The registered
comparison is $\mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S2}) - \mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S1})$ over two runs each,
and that estimand remains incomplete (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). With Qwen2.5-VL-3B under
QLoRA and the present automatic labeling pipeline, descriptor-first stays below its
sentence-only control. The same checkpoint sits $+9.6$ pp $[+8.2, +11.1]$ above the untuned
model.

\subsection{A preference objective at the descriptor layer}
\label{sec:mindesc}
The diagnosis of Section~\ref{sec:diag} below locates identification accuracy as the
constrained variable. The stage-2 objective was registered as a dated amendment after that
diagnosis was seen and before any stage-2 score existed, and it acts at that layer.

MIN-DESC scores $60.0\%$, and CE2, the accepted-only supervised continuation, scores
$59.4\%$. Both procedures start from the same S2 checkpoint and use the same accepted data,
schedule and 800 optimizer updates, while MIN additionally processes a rejected response. The
implemented-procedure contrast is $+0.63$ pp, below the $2.11$ pp this design can resolve
with one run per branch. That threshold combines a paired instrument error of $0.38$ pp on a
stage-2 pair with a seed term of $0.46$ pp, and is not comparable to the $2.2$ pp quoted for
S1$-$Base, whose instrument error is twice as large. Of the rise from S2 to MIN-DESC, $78\%$ is the supervised
continuation (+2.24 of +2.87 pp), not the preference-procedure contrast.

At one run per branch, $\mathrm{MIN}-\mathrm{CE2}$ is $+0.63$ pp, $[+0.16, +1.10]$, with
$b = 42$, $c = 70$, $\chi^2 = 6.5$ and $p = 0.011$. That interval carries instrument noise
only; its cluster-bootstrap standard error is $0.24$ pp. A steeper split appears at the
descriptor layer. The accepted-only continuation lifts joint name-and-point accuracy by $+5.9$ pp against
$+0.8$ pp for the preference procedure. The division is $88/12$, measured on the $3{,}473$
gold-name steps, the $3{,}245$ of Table~\ref{tab:cross} plus the $228$ that emit no parsable
descriptor, without calling the grounder.

$\mathrm{MIN}-\mathrm{S1/101}$ is $+0.94$ pp, $[-0.09, +2.05]$, $p = 0.11$; against the
two-run S1 mean the gap is $+0.68$ pp. The point estimate
is positive; the interval does not establish a difference.
Under the conventional tolerance rule alone, MIN and S1/101 are level at $69.2$.
$\mathrm{CE2}-\mathrm{S1/101}$ is $+0.31$ pp, $p = 0.61$.

\emph{A registered variant stopped by its feasibility check.} Two sources for the rejected
side were registered: the heuristic one used here and an on-policy one taking the element
the checkpoint itself names when it names the wrong one.

The on-policy source failed its pre-registered feasibility gate: 14,000 training screens
yielded 459 usable pairs, $3.3\%$ against a registered $25\%$; on the most permissive
denominator, the wrong-name steps alone, the yield is $14.1\%$. On the 3,246 wrong-name training steps with a parsable point, the
distance quartiles were 70, 351 and 748 px; an 80--350 px window rejected 76.5\%. We did not
test why the preference procedure moved identification so little. The heuristic negative
source is one possibility; the 800-update budget, the objective and the backbone are others,
and this design cannot separate them.

\label{sec:diag}

registered before any treatment score existed, the activation slice of
Section~\ref{sec:noslice} and the strict canonicalization of Section~\ref{sec:alt}. Together
they identify the variable a follow-up objective could target.

\subsection{Concentration of the difference in \texorpdfstring{$7.3\%$}{7.3\%} of steps}
\label{sec:noslice}
The slice separating steps that emit a descriptor from those that do not was registered on
19 August, before any S2 score existed. On the $4{,}138$ steps with a descriptor, S1 and S2 read
$62.2$ and $60.8$ ($\Delta = -1.38$, $[-2.54, -0.19]$); on the $325$ with none, $19.7$ and $10.8$
($\Delta = -8.92$, $[-12.57, -5.25]$). Of the $4{,}138$ emitting a descriptor, $4{,}126$ parse;
the other $12$ join the $325$ to make $337$ steps with no parsable descriptor, which
Table~\ref{tab:cross} cannot cross; the $881$ without a gold name fall outside it as well. The $92.7\%$ carrying a descriptor contribute
$-1.28$ pp of the total against $-0.65$ pp from the $7.3\%$ without one. That slice therefore
carries $34\%$ of the difference. There the model misjudges the \emph{action class}, though references
score $68.3\%$, so the steps are solvable. The untuned model names the correct action class on
$83.4\%$ of them against $55.1\%$ for the control. The branch itself reaches $38.8\%$, below both. The reference for one such step reads
\emph{Click on the close icon} while the branch writes \emph{Swipe up}. The fall
coincides with fine-tuning and continues under the descriptor-first target, on a slice with
no descriptor. These rates compare
the canonicalized action class of each generated sentence with the reference. These
$325$ steps are defined by the treatment's own behavior, yet the control also scores $19.7\%$.

\subsection{Four alternative explanations tested and set aside}
\label{sec:alt}
\emph{Length.} The difference is negative on long ($-1.85$) and on short ($-2.00$) sentences.
\emph{Application slice.} It is negative on seen ($-2.25$), unseen ($-6.41$, $n = 78$) and
unmapped ($-1.59$) steps alike. \emph{Canonicalization.} The \emph{go back} $\to$ touch collapse
in the action check hides this failure mode. Under the strict variant registered on 16 August,
the difference moves from $-1.93$ to $-1.86$, with the ordering unchanged. \emph{Label noise.}
On the $26$ steps whose descriptor label is OCR garbage the difference is $+7.69$ ($p = 0.63$), the opposite
direction.

\subsection{Implications for the intervention}
Gains reported for grounding-first targets~\cite{gcot} are read on answer accuracy, while
executability runs from a measured $12.0$ to a measured $75.7$; the two scales do not convert
into one another.

The two middle rows of Table~\ref{tab:cross}, $+1.1$ $[-2.5, +4.7]$ for the point alone and
$+0.5$ $[-2.8, +4.3]$ for the name alone, separate neither from zero nor from each other.
Neither slot can be given a verdict. Lifting the fourth row to the control's level on its $738$ steps, membership held fixed, is worth $4.2$ pp arithmetically over the full
$4{,}463$-step population. Section~\ref{sec:mindesc} reports the objective registered for that,
and what it returned.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\emph{An incomplete registered estimand.} The descriptor branch and both stage-2 branches have
one training run each, and the registered attribution controls S2r and S2-nopoint were not run.
Every contrast at the descriptor stage is therefore exploratory, and the registered two-run
quantity is not available from these numbers.

\emph{Blind region.} The human references fail on $1{,}083$ steps, $72\%$ of those because the
grounder lands more than $14\%$ of screen width from the element. Base, S1 and the references
fail together on $935$ steps. The instrument catches a sentence that names the wrong element. It
does not catch a point that lands slightly off inside the right one, being blind below the
radius at which neighboring elements are merged. The error distribution behind that
blindness is bipolar. On steps the grounder resolves, its median offset is $0.4\%$ of screen
width; on steps it misses, the median is $26.2\%$, with little mass in between. The verdict is
settled by the tolerance test before the nearest-center clause refines it.

\emph{Grounder dependence.} Our headline grounder is trained on the AndroidControl
split~\cite{uground}, and both grounders are built on Qwen-family backbones. A register effect
and a same-family effect both remain possible, with no human evaluation anchoring either.
Our paraphrase probes keep the element name verbatim, so they test phrasing rather than the
referring expression that~\cite{groundersunderstand} perturbs; the one probe that does replace
the name substitutes a generic word rather than searching adversarially. Their $84\%$ is the yield of an adversarial search over
Windows desktops, and the mobile case they leave open is ours.

\emph{Split composition.} The split is held out by task, not by application. Of the mapped
applications $95.6\%$ also occur in training, and $59\%$ of scored steps cannot be mapped at all,
unknown, and never read as unseen. Task disjointness is also not phrase
disjointness, since $17.3\%$ of test references recur verbatim in training.

\emph{Scope of the construct.} Every claim is at the level of a system, never of a single
sentence. The one setting in which this family of measures has been validated against people
recommends a reference-based score for ranking systems~\cite{zhao2021}. The instrument asks
whether a grounding model reaches the right element, not whether a person would, and a system
could climb it by writing for the grounder. The floor controls of Section~\ref{sec:metric} bound how far
a system could climb that way; they do not rule it out.

\emph{External validity.} Every result here is confined to a single screen, English interfaces,
one dataset, one 3B base model and one training configuration. Scoring is teacher-forced on
human-written history, so absolute levels do not transfer to a system running its own trajectory.

\section{Conclusion}

We studied two controlled target-space training interventions for GUI instruction
generation: a descriptor-first target and descriptor-controlled ORPO pairs. Sentence-only
fine-tuning reaches $59.4\%$ executability averaged over two training runs, up from $47.6\%$
(human-reference score $75.7\%$, $n=4{,}463$). Descriptor-first scores $57.2\%$; the stage-2
procedures score $60.0\%$ for MIN-DESC and $59.4\%$ for its accepted-only supervised
continuation. Executability is the scoring rule, not the model contribution.

On the single run available, the descriptor-first target scores below its sentence-only
control with the backbone, prompt and decoding rule held fixed, and the sign holds under a
second grounder not trained on AndroidControl. Its cost is located and measured, with
$7.3\%$ of steps carrying $34\%$ of the difference and the fourth row of
Table~\ref{tab:cross} worth $4.2$ pp against its control. The variable a follow-up would move, identification accuracy, is readable on the $3{,}473$ gold-name steps without
calling a grounder.

The S2$-$S1 difference is positive when the branch's own descriptor is correct and turns
negative once it is not (Table~\ref{tab:cross}). Further runs of the descriptor stages, and methods that raise identification accuracy,
are the natural next experiments.
