\begin{abstract}
A GUI agent outputs a coordinate for a machine to click. We train a model for a different
consumer: a person who must be told, in one sentence, which on-screen element to touch.
Two questions follow. Does supervision built automatically from recorded episodes teach a
small vision-language model to write such sentences, and does it help to make the model
\emph{name the target element in a structured slot before writing the sentence}? We
fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B with QLoRA on $64{,}567$ automatically labelled steps and score
$4{,}463$ held-out touch steps by \emph{executability}: the sentence goes to an
independent grounding model that never sees the gold coordinate, and the step is correct
when the returned point falls in the cell of the element the user touched. The first
answer is affirmative and replicated --- $47.6\%$ to $59.4\%$ over two seeds, against a
human-reference level of $75.7\%$ and a contentless-sentence floor of $12.0\%$, the gap
being $22\times$ the seed-to-seed noise and keeping its sign under a second, independently
trained grounder. The second cannot be completed: the descriptor-first branch scores
$57.2\%$ at its single seed, $1.93$ points below the matched control, and the
pre-registered two-seed estimand was abandoned for budget reasons, leaving the comparison
inside the band declared inconclusive in advance. What we can report is a mechanism.
Crossing the model's own descriptor against gold labels, the component adds $5.7$ points
where it identifies the element correctly and subtracts $25.2$ where it does not, and the
human references still reach $66.3\%$ in that failing cell, so difficulty alone does not
account for the collapse. A slice registered before any treatment score
localises a third of the difference in the $7.3\%$ of steps emitting no descriptor at all,
and there the culprit is fine-tuning itself: the untuned model names the correct action
class on $83.4\%$ of them against $55.1\%$ and $38.8\%$ for the two trained branches.
Structured-prefix supervision, on this evidence, does not fail uniformly; its value is
governed by identification accuracy, and it amplifies an error the fine-tuning stage
introduces.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Given a mobile screenshot and a user goal, a GUI agent predicts an action such as
\texttt{click(872,141)}. A different consumer of the same input is a person who needs
to be told what to do: \emph{tap the pencil icon at the top right of the note}.
Systems serving this second consumer are appearing in accessibility and user-support
settings~\cite{guideme}, but the generation problem has had little quantitative
treatment, and the reason is that the output is a sentence. A recorded touch
coordinate can be compared with a predicted one; a sentence cannot be compared with a
single reference, because many wordings identify the same button and no dataset
enumerates them.

This paper is a model study, and it needs an instrument before it can be one. We use
\emph{executability}: the generated sentence goes to a separate grounding
model~\cite{uground} that never sees the gold coordinate, and the step counts as
correct when that model lands in the cell of the element the user touched and the
action class agrees. Section~\ref{sec:metric} reports the calibration that licenses
its use here --- an adequacy gate, a measured ceiling, a measured floor, and a
sensitivity analysis over five hit rules --- in the compressed form a model paper
needs; the instrument itself is characterised at length elsewhere.

Two model questions are then asked in the order they should be asked.

\emph{Does automatically constructed supervision work at all?} We build training
targets from AndroidControl~\cite{androidcontrol} with no human annotation and no
labels generated by another language model, and fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B~\cite{qwen25vl}
with QLoRA~\cite{lora,qlora}. It does: $47.6 \to 59.4$ points, at two seeds, with the
gap surviving six alternative explanations, one of which discards every sentence that
reproduces its reference verbatim.

\emph{Does it help to name the element before speaking?} The referring-expression
tradition says a description is good when a listener recovers the referent from
it~\cite{mao2016,luo2017,yu2017}, and the residual errors of the fine-tuned model are
overwhelmingly of that kind: of its $1{,}824$ failures, $86\%$ carry the correct action
and a wording the grounder cannot resolve. So we registered a branch whose target is a
structured descriptor --- role, name, coordinate, distinguishing cue --- followed by
the same scored sentence, byte for byte, as the control branch. This is the ablation
the paper is built around, and the honest report of it has three parts. The registered
estimand averages two seeds per branch and one of the four runs was cancelled for
budget reasons, permanently; at the single seed available the treatment sits $1.93$
points below its control, inside the band the pre-registration declared inconclusive.
The mechanism, however, is legible, and Section~\ref{sec:diag} spends its space there:
within the strata it defines, the component works where the model identifies the element
correctly and costs heavily where it does not, and the largest single locus of that cost
is a failure fine-tuning introduces before any descriptor is involved. None of this is a
statement about the registered quantity, which stays incomplete.

Our contributions are: (i) a fully automatic supervision pipeline for GUI instruction
generation, with the resulting fine-tuning gain measured at two seeds and replicated
under a second grounder; (ii) a pre-registered ablation of descriptor-first
supervision, reported against its own registered reading rules including the case
where those rules refuse a conclusion; and (iii) a diagnosis, exploratory and at one
seed, that separates what the structured prefix does from what fine-tuning does, and
that names the specific failure a follow-up objective would have to repair.

\section{Related Work}

\emph{GUI grounding and agents.} AndroidControl~\cite{androidcontrol} provides
$15{,}283$ human-recorded episodes with per-step instructions and gold touch
coordinates; AndroidInTheWild~\cite{aitw} is the source of the $14\%$ tolerance
convention. Grounding models such as SeeClick~\cite{seeclick}, OS-Atlas~\cite{osatlas},
UGround~\cite{uground} and UI-Venus~\cite{uivenus} map a text expression to a screen
point; we use two of them as instruments, not as baselines. In Aguvis~\cite{aguvis} a
model emits a low-level natural-language instruction, but only as an intermediate
representation en route to an action, and its quality is never scored. In
AndroidControl the human-written per-step instruction is an \emph{input} that
conditions action prediction; here it is the generation target and the only scored
output. Supplying a structured list of screen elements is likewise established --- the
AndroidControl authors fine-tune with an accessibility-derived element list as input,
Mind2Web~\cite{mind2web} trains over noisy candidates, Widget
Captioning~\cite{widgetcap} puts the view hierarchy at the input --- so we make no
novelty claim for that layer.

\emph{Discriminative description.} Producing a description that identifies one object
among distractors is referring-expression generation~\cite{mao2016}, and later work
places a comprehension model inside the training loop to enforce
discriminativeness~\cite{luo2017,yu2017}. The closest supervised work in the GUI domain
is Widget Captioning~\cite{widgetcap}, which identifies the problem of visually
identical widgets but trains with plain cross-entropy. Our claim is correspondingly
narrow: discriminativeness placed in the \emph{training target} for GUI instructions,
and measured. Outside the GUI domain, targets that make a model emit grounding
coordinates before answering have been reported to help when they are \emph{trained}
rather than prompted~\cite{gcot}, which is the closest prior to our branch and the
expectation against which our result should be read. The nearest evidence inside the GUI
domain points the same way: Aguvis reports that removing the inner monologue from its
training targets costs $11.4$ points on AndroidControl-Low~\cite{aguvis}. That result is
about an intermediate representation consumed by an action head, not about a sentence
handed to a person, so it sets an expectation for our branch without settling it.

\emph{Reference-free evaluation.} Verifying generated text against an external
structured source rather than the generator's own beliefs is the principle behind
ALOHa~\cite{aloha}; perturbation checklists with pre-declared thresholds are an
established way of validating a metric~\cite{sai2021}. Two cautions apply directly:
automatic proxies for human judgement are easily over-trusted~\cite{clark2021}, and
grounder accuracy measured on one ``best'' instruction per element overstates what
these models can do~\cite{groundersunderstand}. The term \emph{executability} is used
differently in open grounded planning~\cite{ogp}, where it is the share of plans whose
every action appears in a given action library --- a symbolic lookup over text. Ours is
behavioural and visual.

\section{Task, Supervision, and Branches}
\label{sec:data}

The task is: given one screenshot $I$ and a user goal $q$, produce a single instruction
$s$ in natural language identifying the element to be touched and the action to
perform, addressed to a human reader.

\emph{Supervision.} No public redistribution of AndroidControl carries the
human-written instruction, the screenshot and the accessibility tree together, so we
join two mirrors on \texttt{(episode\_id, step\_id)} and take accessibility trees for
$99{,}131$ screens from the same release. An off-by-one join would corrupt every
downstream label while leaving the pipeline functional, so it is tested: OCR text at
the gold touch point appears in the reference instruction on $48\%$ of $n=400$ sampled
steps against $20\%$ under a deliberately shifted control. The corpus holds $64{,}567$
training and $6{,}958$ test steps over $12{,}895$ and $1{,}432$ tasks, about $64\%$ of
each being touch steps and forming the scoring population; the split is held out by
task with zero task overlap, verified at full scale. Only $12.6\%$ of accessibility
elements carry any name~\cite{chen2020}, so every screenshot is passed through OCR,
used both as an input feature and as a name source. The descriptor label at each touch
step is built by rule from the smallest accessibility box containing the gold point:
role from the tree, name from the tree when it passes a validity gate and from OCR
inside the box otherwise, the gold point on a $[0,1000]$ grid, and a distinguishing cue.
At full scale $73.6\%$ of labels carry a clear name, $22.0\%$ none, and $7.6\%$ of
target elements share their name with another element on the same screen. The
construction, its two filtering gates and its label-quality audit are the subject of a
companion paper~\cite{companionvcl}; this paper uses the output and reports only what
constrains the model results.

\emph{Branches.} Four datasets are built from identical inputs and differ only in the
generation target: \textbf{S1}, the sentence alone; \textbf{S2}, descriptor then
sentence; \textbf{S2r}, a false descriptor taken from a different screen with
randomised coordinates, token-length matched per example; and \textbf{S2-nopoint}, S2
without the coordinate slot. An S2 target reads
\texttt{<desc>tappable text | Search here | <point>440,91</point> | just above the text
"Coffee"</desc>} followed by the same scored sentence as S1. Nine structural
invariants are checked after every rebuild and all nine hold at full scale; the one
that matters most here is that the scored sentence is byte-identical across branches on
all $64{,}567$ examples, so a difference between branches is a difference in what
precedes the sentence and in nothing else. S2r and S2-nopoint were registered as
attribution controls and, for the budget reasons of Section~\ref{sec:design}, were not
run; we mark them as registered-and-unrun rather than dropping them from the record.

\section{Descriptor-First Supervision}
\label{sec:method}

The intervention is a change of target, not of architecture, input or inference cost.
Both branches read the same prompt --- goal, the reference instructions of the previous
three steps, and the OCR strings --- and both are scored on the sentence alone, with any
descriptor stripped before scoring. S2 differs only in that the target begins with four
slots: \emph{role} (button, text field, list item), \emph{name} (the displayed string),
\emph{coordinate} (the gold touch point rescaled to $[0,1000]$), and a
\emph{distinguishing cue} (the nearest OCR string outside the box with its relative
direction).

Two design choices need stating because they are not free. First, the coordinate slot
is included: it is a perfectly clean label at zero construction cost, and the evidence
available at design time indicated that intermediate steps carrying an explicit point
help where free-form verbal reasoning hurts. Second, the descriptor is emitted, not
consumed: the model must produce its own identification before it writes, which is what
makes the branch an ablation of \emph{discriminative commitment} rather than of extra
input. The mechanism a reader should have in mind, and the one Section~\ref{sec:diag}
tests, is that this commitment cuts both ways --- a model that has just written the
right name and the right point is well placed to write a sentence about that element,
and a model that has written the wrong ones has bound itself to them.

\section{The Instrument}
\label{sec:metric}

\subsection{Definition}
Let $s$ be the generated sentence with any descriptor removed, $I$ the screenshot, $g$
the gold touch coordinate, and $E$ the set of accessibility elements on the screen after
merging elements whose centres lie within $24$\,dp. Let $G$ be a grounding model,
independent of the model under test and without access to $g$, returning $p = G(I,s)$.
The step is correct when all three hold: \emph{(i) action}, the canonicalised action
types of $s$ and of the reference agree, twelve surface verbs mapping to one touch class
and touch, type, scroll, long-press and back remaining distinct; \emph{(ii) polarity},
$s$ and the reference do not name opposite states of the same control, checked against
nine toggle pairs; \emph{(iii) localisation}, $p$ lies within the conventional
tolerance $|p_x - g_x| \le 0.14W$, $|p_y - g_y| \le 0.14H$~\cite{aitw} \emph{and} no
competing element centre is closer to $p$ than $g$ itself. The tolerance region is a
per-axis rectangle, not a disc ($\pm151$ px horizontally against $\pm336$ px vertically
on a $1080\times2400$ screen), and the site of the target cell is the recorded touch
point, not the centre of the box containing it. Condition (iii) refines the tolerance
test and never accepts a step that test rejects, so the two are not alternatives.
Executability is the proportion of touch steps satisfying all three.

The rubric is a recorded human touch coordinate, not a model's opinion, so this is not
LLM-as-judge: $G$ contributes a localisation and geometry against $g$ decides. The same
$G$ scores every branch, which removes any bias acting equally on all of them.

Fig.~\ref{fig:cell} shows a step on which the two rules disagree. The choice is not
cosmetic: against a sentence naming the wrong button the tolerance test still awards
credit in $84.3\%$ of cases, a dynamic range of some $16$ points, where the cell rule
awards $2.8\%$ while holding a ceiling of $99.7\%$ under a $3\%$ injected offset. We
report the tolerance score alongside the headline throughout, never as the headline.

\subsection{Calibration}
Four measurements license the readings in this paper.

\emph{Adequacy.} A pass condition of median localisation error at most $3\%$ of screen
width was fixed before any compute was spent, derived from screen geometry: the
tolerance margin --- half the distance from the target to the nearest other element after
merging --- has median $6.2\%$ of width and 10th percentile $3.6\%$. UGround-V1-2B
attains $0.7\%$ on $300$ test steps. The gate passes, but the error distribution is
bimodal (the grounder either lands near the centre or elsewhere entirely), so a
percentile condition would have been the better choice, and one declared
installation-fault detector fired: on $30.7\%$ of steps the horizontal answer is exactly
screen centre, part data and part the grounder abandoning the axis when it cannot
identify an element. That depresses scores rather than inflating them.

\emph{Ceiling.} Passing every human-written reference through the same pipeline scores
$\mathbf{75.7\%}$ $[74.1, 77.3]$ over all $4{,}463$ touch steps. Every score
below is read against $75.7$, not against $100$. An earlier estimate from a $300$-step
subsample gave $70.0\%$, five points low; that number is withdrawn.

\emph{Floor.} Replacing every sentence with the contentless \emph{tap the button} scores
$12.0\%$ $[9.7, 14.4]$ on a random $800$-step slice whose ceiling is $74.9\%$, so the
usable range is $62.9$ points and the metric is not carried by a visual prior. A
genuine reference sentence written for a \emph{different} step --- correct register,
matched length, wrong content --- scores lower still, $6.1\%$ $[4.5, 7.9]$, on disjoint
intervals: a wrong sentence actively misleads the instrument where an empty one leaves
it to a weak prior. This also disposes of the reading that the metric rewards
annotator style, since style is exactly what that control preserves.

\emph{What the metric weighs.} On the same slice, keeping the locative clause and
replacing the element name with the generic \emph{the item} costs $28.5$ points on the
$193$ steps it touches ($b{=}58$, $c{=}3$, $p<0.001$), against $3.5$ points for deleting
the locative clause and keeping the name on a near-identical population of $198$
($b{=}8$, $c{=}1$, $p{=}0.046$). Naming the element is worth roughly eight times saying
where it is. Meaning-preserving rewrites of the references --- verb substitution,
fronting the locative clause, both at once --- move executability by $+0.35$ pp
($[-0.59, +1.29]$) over the $1{,}139$ steps they alter, with $2.6\%$ of steps changing
verdict, so the instrument is insensitive to phrasing while being sensitive to content.

\emph{Why not a reference-based score.} The premise that one reference cannot carry the
judgement is testable on our own outputs. Scored with a content-word F1 against the single
reference, $52.8\%$ of the sentences the metric accepts from the untuned model fall below
$0.5$, against $13.9\%$ for the fine-tuned one: fine-tuning moves wording towards the
annotator's register, so a reference-based score charges the two arms unequally for a
property unrelated to identification. BLEU-4 and ROUGE-L happen to rank our arms in the
same order ($9.7/40.3$ for Base against $38.7/67.3$ for S1), and we draw nothing from that
agreement --- two measures can agree and both be wrong about the property of interest.
What they cannot do is locate the remaining room: the references score $100$ on ROUGE-L
by construction, where executability puts them at $75.7$ and says where the missing
quarter is.

\subsection{Robustness of the instrument}
Choosing a hit rule is a researcher degree of freedom, so we measured what the choice
buys. Rescoring three arms on $698$ steps under five rules --- Euclidean disc, per-axis
rectangle, the cell rule as implemented, a cell rule seeded at the box centre, and a
nearest-box rule --- gives ceilings of $80.2$, $82.2$, $74.2$, $56.6$ and $82.2$ and a
fine-tuning gain of $+12.6$, $+13.0$, $+11.7$, $+9.5$ and $+13.0$ points. The absolute
level moves by up to $26$ points; the ordering never moves and the gain stays between
$9.5$ and $13.0$. The instrument contributes no variance of its own: on the $2{,}810$
steps where two training runs emitted byte-identical sentences the grounder returned
identical coordinates and identical verdicts, $2{,}810$ of $2{,}810$.

Intervals are nonparametric cluster bootstraps with $10{,}000$ resamples clustered by
application ($G = 1{,}091$ clusters, $G_{\text{eff}} = 454.3$ under Kish's
formula~\cite{kish1965}), the clustering rule fixed in advance because it determines the
available power~\cite{cameron2008}. The measured design effect is $1.10$, giving a
minimum detectable paired effect of $2.2$ points at $80\%$ power.

\section{Experimental Design and Pre-Registration}
\label{sec:design}

All branches fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct~\cite{qwen25vl} with
QLoRA~\cite{lora,qlora} under LLaMA-Factory~\cite{llamafactory}: 4-bit NF4, LoRA rank
$8$, $\alpha = 16$, dropout $0.05$, adapters on \texttt{q,k,v,o,gate,up,down}, vision
tower and projector frozen, context $2560$, effective batch $16$, learning rate
$1\times10^{-4}$, cosine schedule, two epochs, on a single A100 ($8{,}072$ steps,
$\approx 23$ h). One configuration file serves every branch and three keys change in it
--- dataset, seed, output directory --- each run archiving the file it used; the
trainable parameter count was verified arithmetically at $14{,}966{,}784$, which
confirms the vision tower stayed frozen. Losses on an L4 and an A100 agree to three
digits with identical \texttt{total\_flos}, and generation at batch size $1$ and $8$ is
byte-identical.

Inference is greedy in every arm, one sentence per step, with the descriptor stripped by a
fixed regular expression before scoring so that no arm is scored on text the others cannot
produce; the untuned arm reads the same prompt template as the trained ones. The prompt
supplies the goal, the human-written instructions of the previous three steps, and the OCR
strings, and is byte-identical across branches and between training and inference. Because
the history field carries human-written sentences rather than an action log, scoring is
teacher-forced on context: absolute scores are conditioned on that, while differences
between branches are not, the field being a constant of the experiment.

The design was committed to version control before any training run. It fixes the
reading of all four outcomes in advance: a $\Delta$ whose interval excludes zero and
exceeds the seed noise floor is positive; one whose interval touches zero is a trend;
an interval covering zero with $|\Delta|$ below the detectable effect is inconclusive;
a negative upper bound is harm. The registered quantity is
$\Delta = \mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S2}) - \mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S1})$ over \emph{two seeds
per branch}, with the operative threshold set at $2.8$ pp --- the bootstrap standard
error of the paired difference, $0.38$ pp, combined with a seed term of $0.46$ pp
doubled because it rests on one degree of freedom --- and the inconclusive band
correspondingly at $-2.8 \ldots +1.7$ pp. Amendments are appended with dates and never
edited in place; twenty-seven are recorded, twenty-one of them before any executability
score existed. We flag rather than hide the pattern in the six that came later: each
moved the room upward and the threshold downward, and the first of them also moved
against us, cutting the share of the ceiling reached by the control branch from $84.4\%$
to $78.4\%$.

One departure from the registered plan is not a matter of interpretation and is stated
here rather than in a footnote. \textbf{The second seed of the treatment branch was
never run}, a budget decision taken on 23 August 2026, and it will not be run. The
registered estimand is therefore permanently incomplete, and no later analysis in this
paper upgrades the single-seed comparison into the conclusion the two-seed rule would
have licensed.

\subsection{Automatic supervision works, and the effect is replicated}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the picture. Fine-tuning on supervision that cost no human
annotation moves the model from $47.6$ to $59.4$ points, closing $42\%$ of the distance
to the human-reference level. Paired over the same steps (McNemar), S1 over Base is
$+11.5$ points at seed 101 ($b = 284$, $c = 798$, $\chi^2 = 243.2$) and $+12.0$ at seed
202, both $p<0.001$, where $b$ counts steps the lower arm resolves and the higher one
does not.

All rates are over the same denominator of $4{,}463$ touch steps. Two runs each emitted
one empty sentence --- S1 on one step, S2 on a different one --- and those are counted as
failures rather than dropped, since discarding a model's own refusal to answer would
remove exactly the cases the metric exists to catch; every paired test is therefore run
over the full population with a missing answer scored zero.

That number is flattered by the quarter of steps on which S1 reproduces the reference's
content words: restricted to the $3{,}367$ steps where the model writes its own
sentence, the arms read $44.7$, $53.1$ and $75.0$ and the fraction closed is $28\%$,
with the gap still $+8.4$ points ($b=264$, $c=547$, $p<0.001$). Five further
alternative readings were tested on the per-step record and none reverses the ordering:
scoring localisation alone leaves $+11.3$ points, and the untuned model in fact names
the correct action class \emph{more} often ($96.5\%$ against $94.4\%$); the gain is
$+15.0$ points on a task's opening step against $+11.0$ afterwards, on overlapping
intervals; screen difficulty by element count gives $+12.6$, $+9.5$, $+12.4$;
per-cluster, S1 wins $395$ clusters, ties $565$ and loses $131$, reproduced at the second
seed; and there is no home-field advantage, S1 scoring $59.1\%$ on applications seen in
training, $59.0\%$ on unseen ones and $59.2\%$ where none can be attributed.

\emph{Retraining noise.} A gap between systems is only meaningful against the movement
caused by rebuilding one system. Retraining S1 under a second seed shifts the score by
$+0.5$ points, a paired interval of $[-0.2, +1.3]$ containing zero, McNemar $p = 0.19$.
The sharper contrast is churn: the two seeds disagree on $6.4\%$ of steps where the
fine-tuning intervention moves $24.2\%$. Step-level agreement across seeds is $93.6\%$
($\kappa = 0.867$), but $63\%$ of those pairs are byte-identical sentences on which a
deterministic instrument must agree; on the $1{,}652$ steps where the two runs actually
wrote something different it is $82.6\%$ ($\kappa = 0.650$), and that is the figure we
quote.

\subsection{The descriptor branch}
The registered comparison is $\mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S2}) - \mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{S1})$
over two seeds each. With the treatment branch at one seed, what can be stated is this:
\emph{with Qwen2.5-VL-3B under QLoRA and the present automatic labelling pipeline, the
S2/101 checkpoint scores $1.93$ points below S1/101 ($b = 254$, $c = 340$, interval
$[-3.06, -0.75]$) and $2.44$ points below S1/202 over the full $4{,}463$ steps, the sign
holds under UI-Venus, and the registered estimand remains incomplete.} Against the
declared bands, $-2.19$ pp relative to the two-seed control mean falls inside
$-2.8 \ldots +1.7$: \emph{inconclusive}, not a negative result, and the missing run means
it stays there. The seed noise measured on the control pair, $\pm1.3$ pp at the interval
edge, is itself most of the reason: a single run can move by half the observed
difference.

It is worth stating what the missing run could and could not have changed, since the
arithmetic is fixed by the registered thresholds. For the two-seed mean to reach the
weakly-positive band the second treatment run would have to score $65.0\%$, which is
$5.4$ points above the best checkpoint we have measured and above the second grounder's
ceiling on its own slice; for it to reach the negative band the run would have to score
at or below $56.0\%$, which is well inside the range a seed change can produce. The
outcome space that remained was therefore \emph{inconclusive or negative}, and the
budget decision closed it at inconclusive. We report this rather than let a reader infer
a suppressed positive.

Two things are nevertheless established for this branch and do not depend on the missing
seed. It is far above the untuned model, $+9.6$ points $[+8.2, +11.1]$, so the
descriptor target does not break fine-tuning; and it emits its coordinate slot well,
$71.0\%$ of self-generated points landing inside the $14\%$ window with a median error of
$0.81\%$ of screen width horizontally. Whatever the branch costs, it does not cost the
ability to point.

\section{Where the Descriptor Helps and Where It Breaks}
\label{sec:diag}

This section is exploratory and rests on one treatment seed. It is reported because the
mechanism it exposes is what a follow-up objective would have to act on, and because
two of its components were registered before any treatment score existed.

\subsection{The component works when identification succeeds}
The branch emits its own descriptor, so its identification can be scored directly
against the gold labels, independently of the sentence. Crossing name correctness against
coordinate correctness on the $3{,}240$ steps carrying a gold name gives
Table~\ref{tab:cross}.

The branch wins or ties in three cells of four. All of the damage sits in one: when the
model has written both the wrong name and a coordinate outside tolerance --- $738$ steps,
$16.5\%$ of the population --- its executability falls to $5.6\%$. The reading is that the
mechanism does what it was designed to do, and that its cost is commitment: having named
the wrong element, the branch writes a sentence faithful to that element, while the
control, having committed to nothing, still retrieves the right one three times in ten.

The obvious objection is that this stratification conditions on a variable the treatment
itself produces, so the failing cell may simply be the hard steps. The two rightmost
columns are there to test that, and they are not conditioned on the treatment. If the
cell were merely hard, the human references would fall with it; they reach $66.3\%$
there, $9$ points below their own population average, where the treatment reaches $5.6$.
The untuned model, which has no descriptor mechanism at all, reaches $24.4$. So the cell
is somewhat harder than average and that does not account for the collapse. The
comparison is not clean --- the references were never conditioned on anything, and a
correctly specified test would need a difficulty measure fixed before the treatment ran
--- but the confound is bounded rather than merely acknowledged. One cell does behave as
the objection predicts: \emph{name correct, point outside}, where the ceiling itself is
$31.0\%$, is a region the instrument largely cannot see, and the near-zero $\Delta$ there
should be read as absence of signal rather than absence of effect.

The decomposition of the total is arithmetic and carries no such caveat: $-1.28$ pp comes
from the $92.7\%$ of steps carrying a descriptor and $-0.63$ pp from the $7.3\%$ that do
not.

not was registered on 19 August, before any S2 score existed.

The $7.3\%$ of steps where the branch emits nothing carry $34\%$ of the total difference.
Those steps are where the model misjudges the \emph{action class}: the reference says
\emph{Click on the close icon} and the branch writes \emph{Swipe up}. Three readings
follow, and only the third is speculative. The human references score $68.3\%$ on these
same steps, so they are solvable and the instrument is not blind there. The failure is
not created by the descriptor: the untuned model names the correct action class on
$83.4\%$ of them, against $55.1\%$ for the control branch and $38.8\%$ for the treatment.
Fine-tuning is what damages the action prior --- the untuned model is better than
\emph{both} trained branches at this --- and the descriptor target amplifies the damage
rather than causing it. The mechanism we cannot yet test is that descriptors appear only
on touch steps in training, so the model may have learnt the shortcut ``descriptor
$\Leftrightarrow$ touch'' and, when unsure, drop the descriptor and commit to a
non-touch sentence. The branch registered to test exactly that, S2-nopoint, is among the
runs the budget cancelled.

One limitation must travel with this slice: the $325$ steps are defined by the
treatment's own behaviour, so ``the treatment does badly there'' is partly definitional.
What is not definitional is that the control also scores only $19.7\%$ there, so the
region is genuinely hard, and that the references reach $68.3\%$.

\subsection{Four alternative explanations tested and set aside}
\emph{Length.} The difference is negative on long sentences ($-1.85$) and short ones
($-2.00$), and the length distributions of the two branches coincide (median $31$
characters each). \emph{Application slice.} Negative on seen ($-2.25$), unseen ($-6.41$)
and unattributable ($-1.59$) steps alike. \emph{Canonicalisation.} The known
\emph{go back} $\to$ touch collapse in condition (i) hides exactly this failure mode ---
$64$ treatment sentences containing \emph{go back} pass the action gate where only $21$
are genuinely executable --- so we re-ran with the strict variant, registered on 16
August before any treatment score: the difference moves from $-1.93$ to $-1.86$ and all
four arms drop by $0.20$--$0.31$ points with the ordering unchanged. \emph{Label noise.}
On the $26$ steps whose descriptor label is OCR garbage the difference is $+7.69$
($p = 0.63$), the opposite direction.

\subsection{Why this does not simply contradict the grounding-first prior}
Results reporting gains for targets that ground before answering are not measured on
this task, and three differences are large enough to matter. Their supervision is a
question--answer pair in which the grounded object is what the question is
\emph{about}, so an error in the grounding step is visible in the answer; ours is a
free-form instruction in which a wrong descriptor still admits a fluent, wrong sentence.
Their grounding target is verified against annotation at training time only, whereas our
descriptor is scored at inference against the model's own identification, which is where
Table~\ref{tab:cross} finds the asymmetry. And their reported gains are measured on
answer accuracy, a metric with a floor near chance, while executability has a measured
floor of $12.0$ and a measured ceiling of $75.7$, leaving $62.9$ points of usable range
in which a $2$-point movement is small. Our result is best read as a boundary condition
on that prior rather than as a contradiction of it: the prefix pays where the model can
identify the referent, and identification on a mobile screen with $22.0\%$ of target
elements carrying no name at all is harder than it is for a photographed object.

\subsection{What this says about the intervention}
Read together, the three tables say something more specific than ``the ablation did not
pay''. Descriptor-first supervision is not uniformly inert: it adds $5.7$ points where
the model identifies the element correctly, which is most of the population, and it
subtracts $25$ where identification has already failed. Its net effect is therefore
governed by identification accuracy, not by the presence of the prefix. The lever a
follow-up should pull is accordingly not ``remove a slot from the descriptor'' --- the
coordinate slot is the one carrying weight, worth $+1.1$ points on its own where the name
is wrong, against $+0.5$ for the name alone --- but ``make the identification step
reliable''. Lifting the failing cell merely to the control's own level on those $738$
steps would be worth $4.2$ points, which is the size of effect the whole study is
looking for. We have pre-registered such an objective, a
minimal-pair preference objective at the descriptor layer trained from the S2 checkpoint,
with its own thresholds and stopping gates fixed in advance; it has no score at the time
of writing and we make no claim for it here. Choosing it \emph{after} seeing the S2
result is itself a post-hoc decision and is recorded as one.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\emph{The registered estimand is incomplete, permanently.} The treatment branch has one
seed. Everything said about it is exploratory, and the sentences forbidden by the
pre-registration --- that the main hypothesis was refuted, that the treatment is harmful
by the registered quantity, that a McNemar $p$-value settles a question in which seed
variance is a component --- are not said here.

\emph{Ceiling and blind region.} The human-reference level is $75.7\%$, so the room for
any intervention is narrower than a score read against $100$ suggests. The references
themselves fail on $1{,}083$ steps, $71\%$ of those because the grounder misses by more
than the tolerance on a human sentence, and $935$ steps defeat all three arms. The
metric also cannot see displacements below about $63$ px, the merge radius, so it detects
naming the wrong element and does not detect pointing slightly off inside the right one.

\emph{Grounder dependence.} Two grounders now agree on the ordering, but both are built
on Qwen-family backbones, so a same-family effect is not excluded, and no human
evaluation anchors either. On the paraphrase side, the rewrites we ran keep the element
name verbatim, so we have probed phrasing and not the referring expression itself, which
is what~\cite{groundersunderstand} perturbs; that work's $84\%$ figure is the yield of an
adversarial search over Windows desktop screens, not a failure rate under ordinary
paraphrase, and the mobile case it leaves open is ours.

\emph{Split composition.} The test split is held out by task, not by application:
$95.6\%$ of attributable applications also occur in training, and $55\%$ of test steps
cannot be attributed to an application at all --- \emph{unknown}, which we never read as
\emph{unseen}. Comparisons against an external model would carry a home-field advantage
and none is reported as a standalone result. Task disjointness is also not phrase
disjointness: $17.3\%$ of test references appear verbatim in a $2.6\%$ slice of the
training data.

\emph{Scope of the construct.} Every claim is at the level of a system, never of a single
sentence, and the one setting where this family of measure has been validated against
people recommends a reference-based score for ranking systems even while its
reference-free model correlates best per instruction~\cite{zhao2021}. The metric asks
whether a grounding model reaches the right element, not whether a person would. A
system could in principle climb it by writing for the grounder; that is bounded but not
excluded --- a sentence naming a screen region scores $89.5\%$ when the region is right
and $3.3\%$ when wrong, so the reward is for being right about position rather than for
mentioning it, and the treatment branch, trained to emit coordinates, uses coordinate
strings in its scored sentences at the references' own rate.

\emph{Scope.} Single screen, English interfaces, one dataset, one base model of 3B
parameters, one training configuration.

\section{Conclusion}

We built supervision for GUI instruction generation without human annotation and
measured what it buys: $47.6 \to 59.4$ points of executability at two seeds, against a
human-reference level of $75.7$ and a contentless floor of $12.0$, with the effect
$22\times$ the seed-to-seed noise and its sign preserved under a second grounder trained
on different data. We then asked whether making the model name the element before
writing the sentence adds anything. The pre-registered answer requires two seeds per
branch and one run was cancelled; at the single seed available the treatment sits $1.93$
points below its control, inside the band declared inconclusive in advance, and it stays
there. The diagnosis is the part we would carry forward: the structured prefix is worth
$+5.7$ points where the model identifies the element correctly and $-25$ where it does
not, and the largest single locus of damage is an action-prior failure that fine-tuning
introduces before any descriptor exists --- the untuned model gets those steps right more
often than either trained branch. Whether a training objective aimed squarely at
identification reliability can convert the first number into a net gain is the
experiment this diagnosis specifies, and it is not yet run.
